The Modern Problem: “Are We Living For Likes?”

By Yousif Omar

Modern society has become increasingly obsessed with consuming praise, digital likes, reactions and the need of superficial things. This is causing a serious decline in productivity and athleticism the incline of valuable attention and shortened attention span. This is shown through quote in the novel The Little Prince when Antoine de Saint-Exupéry mentions “But the conceited man didn’t hear him. Conceited men only hear praise.”

The word "only hear" emphasises the conceited man's constant obsession for admiration, suggesting that he abstracts anything that doesn't suit his need. His behavior reveals how demanding the praise is although unnecessary, it can prevent individuals from appreciating the substantial values of communication and experiences. Upon closer inspection, this also refers to modern social media, as it prompts society to seek approval/validation via likes, followers and positive comments.

Furthermore, some individuals might be driven on earning digital approval (as it might be artificial) to neglect essential responsibilities, resulting in procrastination and reduced productivity. Moreover, Constant comparison of themselves with others online is contributing to increase of anxiety and dissatisfaction. The endless stream of short form content makes attention more valuable and harder to get it, and it makes more difficult for people to maintain their attention on longer tasks. Correspondingly, the excessive time consumed leaves lower opportunities for physical activities.

Through the conceited man, Antoine de Saint-Exupéry criticises the superficial desire for admiration and praise exposing the limitations of judging importance based on the bias of society. His portrayal remains relevant to this day since digital popularity has diminished attention and it can appear more valuable than personal growth, inseparable relationships and real life achievements. Therefore, The Little Prince reminds readers the self-worth shouldn't be dependant on external validation. Rather than seeking praise, individuals should focus on developing their abilities, building deep emotional bonds, and seeing the world through the heart rather than the eyes.

The Conceited Man: The Need for Approval

By Maher Shareef

In The Little Prince by Antoine de Saint-Exupéry, the Little Prince visits a planet inhabited by a conceited man who believes that everyone should admire him. When the Little Prince arrives, the man asks him to clap so that he can bow and lift his hat as a sign of appreciation. He enjoys receiving praise, but he shows little interest in understanding the Little Prince or building a meaningful friendship with him. Through this character, the author shows how vanity can prevent people from forming real connections with others.

The conceited man represents people who are so focused on themselves that they depend on others to feel important. He wants to be admired, yet he never questions whether he deserves the attention he demands. The fact that he lives alone on his planet makes his behavior even more ironic because he wants to be seen as special without having anyone to compare himself to. Through this example, the author encourages readers to question the importance people place on praise and public attention.

This idea is still relevant today, especially in schools and social groups. Some people try to appear more talented, popular, or successful so that others will admire them. They may hide their mistakes or agree with everyone just to fit in. Like the conceited man, they risk caring more about others’ opinions than their own values.

However, receiving praise is not necessarily harmful. Encouragement can help people feel confident and appreciated. The problem begins when a person’s self-worth depends entirely on other people’s opinions. The author reminds us that real value comes from our character, actions, and relationships rather than the attention we receive. Ultimately, the story teaches us to develop confidence in ourselves instead of constantly seeking approval from others.

Social ColumnistArticle 3 · The Psychological Trap

Social Approval: The Psychological Trap

By Yehia Islam

In my opinion, the little prince by Antoine de Saint-exupery teaches us an important lesson about the psychology trap and how people think and behave. I believe the story shows how people can become trapped by their own thoughts and focus too much on things that do not really matter. For example, the king wants to control everything, the businessman is obsessed with owning stars, and the vain man always wants people to admire him. I think these characters represent people in real life who care too much about power, money, and what others think of them. They forget to enjoy life and appreciate the people around them.

From my point of view, the Little Prince is different because he tries to understand the world and discover what makes life meaningful. One part of the story that I find important is his relationship with the rose. At first, he does not fully understand her, but later he realizes that she is special to him because he has spent time caring for her. This makes me understand that love is not only about words but also about the time and effort we give to others. I also think the fox teaches a valuable lesson about friendship because he explains that building a relationship requires patience, trust, and care.

In my opinion, the psychology trap can happen in our daily lives when we compare ourselves to others or try too hard to impress people. Sometimes, we focus on being successful or popular instead of being happy with what we already have. The story reminds me that we should not let society control our thoughts or decide what makes us happy. Instead, we should focus on our values, our friendships, and the people who truly care about us. Overall, I believe the little prince teaches us to look beyond appearances and understand what really matters in life.

Social ColumnistArticle 4 · The Counter-Perspective

The Need Of Approval: Is A Little Social-Approval Really That Bad?

By Ammar Rabie

Although the author of The Little Prince shows that any desire for social approval is simply foolish, refusing all possible forms of social recognition/approval, this ignores the natural and biological human need to be valued, and that applies especially in modern day society where that need is at its absolute peak.

For example, the absolute need for a little social approval in modern day society is specifically highlighted in chapter 4, where the astronomer was completely ignored for wearing regular traditional clothing, but “presented his discovery again in 1920, when he dressed elegantly in European attire and this time everybody believed him.” This shows without a degree of social approval or confirmation from society, brilliance and truth can be completely ignored, this displays that seeking approval from society is not just a foolish reaction, but rather a highly practical tool for respect and acceptance in the world.

Further exploring, while the author was correctly cautious about public judgment, calling the entire desire for recognition foolish is simply too far. In modern day society, gaining positive feedback is a mental catalyst that builds self confidence, increases productivity and further builds social relationships. According to research by the American Psychological Association, positive praise or valuation is needed for healthy mental health. Whether it's a student seeking praise from his teacher or an author seeking praise for his writing, social recognition/approval is a natural biological mechanism designed to keep us connected. It's not an act of foolishness or a sign of moral weakness. While it is true that chasing mindless internet clout and fame is a certain trap, we all need just a little social approval to continue as a functional society. Expecting humans to completely abandon the need for valuation is not only unrealistic, but misunderstands the problem in its entirety.

Social ColumnistArticle 5 · The Future Roadmap

The Future Roadmap: How Do We Stop Living For Likes?

By Omar Adam

In The Little Prince, the conceited man lives alone on a tiny planet and only wants one thing: for someone to clap for him. As soon as the Little Prince arrives, he asks him to clap so he can take off his hat and bow. I think this is a lot like how many of us use social media. We post a photo, then keep checking our phones to see who liked it. If nobody does, we start to feel like the post, or even we ourselves, aren’t good enough.

The fox shows us a better way to think. He tells the Little Prince that “what is essential is invisible to the eye.” To me, this means the things that make a person valuable, like kindness, honesty and loyalty, can’t be counted. Likes and followers can. That’s the problem today. When we measure ourselves with numbers, our confidence goes up and down with every notification, and one post with no likes can make us feel like nobody. The conceited man ends up completely alone, and he doesn’t even seem to mind, because all he wants is applause.

So what can we do in the future? First, I think we should spend more time with the people who actually know us. The fox says friendship is built with time and patience, and no follower can replace that. Second, before we post, we should ask ourselves why we are posting. If it’s because we enjoy it, that’s fine. If it’s only to get attention, maybe we should wait. Third, we should put our energy into things we’re proud of, like a sport, a skill or helping someone, even if nobody sees it.

In conclusion, I believe the Little Prince teaches us to stop trying to impress everybody and start working out who we really are. Once we stop needing a crowd to clap for us, we finally have the time to do that.

Social ColumnistArticle 6 · The Celebrity Trap

The Celebrity Trap: Living in a Glass Planet

By Salman Ahmad

Chasing after social approval is a remarkably tricky trap that has persisted throughout generations. It often ends up feeling like a gilded cage where people trade real life for a constant performance. We see this exact idea play out in The Little Prince, especially with the conceited man on his tiny planet. All he cares about is hearing applause and being admired, but his deep obsession leaves him completely isolated. Surrounded by his own vanity, he is totally cut off from anyone who could offer genuine friendship.

At the end of the day, whether you’re looking at a classic novel or scrolling through social media, the core message remains crystal clear. Chasing the hollow validation of the crowd might look glamorous from the outside, but it is really just a modern prison, trading authentic, messy real-life connections for the temporary applause of people you don’t even know leaves you stranded on your own private island, surrounded by noise yet completely alone.

Fast forward to today, and the celebrity trap shows that modern influencers and public figures are living out the exact same story. They live in a fragile glass planet made of digital screens and endless social media feeds. They perform for millions of strangers every day, letting likes, views and comments dictate their self-worth. But just like the character in the book, being watched by everyone doesn’t mean you aren’t lonely. In fact, it often means the opposite.
